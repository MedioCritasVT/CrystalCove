Opening chapter: The Circle of Inquiry

What this page is for

This page explains a working method behind Conflux: how a small observation can become a question, how that question is given a place to be examined, and how careful interpretation can open possibilities for making something real. The Portal, Atelier, Ledger, and the proposed Node realms are different kinds of surfaces for that work.

It also offers a contemporary reinterpretation of the magic circle. Here, the circle is a useful analogy for a bounded practice of attention, not a newly invented occult tradition or proof of supernatural effects. You do not need to share the symbolism to use the method.

The magic circle, revisited

Across ceremonial traditions, circles and convocations have different meanings and purposes. This page does not claim one universal definition. It considers one practical feature: a ritual draws a boundary around an intention, gives participants a shared frame of reference, and helps distinguish what is explored within that frame from what is acted on outside it.

In psychological terms, the boundary can make room to feel something without immediately obeying it, to represent something without claiming it is literal, and to let several possible meanings coexist. The ritual changes the conditions of attention; the symbols help people navigate that change. That is the comparison Conflux borrows.

Begin with the wisp

The first pass may be no more than a feeling, a frustration, an unexpected pattern, or a question. Notice where it came from and what remains uncertain. There is no demand to turn every passing interest into a plan.

Once there is a reason to look closer, name the goal and draw a boundary around the inquiry: What are we trying to understand? Whose experience matters? What belongs outside this question for now? Only then turn the thought into a prompt that can be interrogated.

Worked example: from uncertainty to the Portal Threshold

This is an illustrative reconstruction of how the method can guide an actual kind of Conflux work: helping somebody find their first step. It uses real Portal surfaces, but does not pretend every question was asked in a single historical session.

  1. Notice the friction. A new visitor sees a game, books, creative tools, and technical downloads but may not know whether they must install everything. Their uncertainty is the starting observation—not a failure to understand.
  2. Bound the question. Set the intention: how can someone enter Conflux, learn what is available, and choose a next step without being pushed into a download or an ideology? That is the circle for this design pass.
  3. Interrogate and format. Ask what a newcomer needs to know, which terms are unfamiliar, what claims can be verified, and which routes should remain optional. Use those questions to format the inquiry before choosing a solution.
  4. Invite different lenses. Medio's lens might ask whether the first step feels welcoming; Ratio's, whether instructions are precise; Nexus's, whether routes remain connected; Specula's, whether claims match evidence; Limen's, whether transitions make sense. These are illustrative questions from the Nodes' design roles, not a claim that this exact meeting occurred.
  5. Translate into surfaces. The answer can take several forms instead of one prescribed route: a Threshold welcome, a Get the Game room, a Player Manual, an Atelier for proposals, and a Ledger for records. Each surface makes a different action possible. Some are present in the local Portal; other wider integrations remain proposals.
  6. Check, decide, revise. Try a first-run walkthrough. Follow each link and distinguish an actual release from a proposed service. If directions are confusing or a link is broken, record the discrepancy and change the design. An exploratory proposal is not an approved feature. Official changes still require the project's real approval and publication steps.
  7. Leave the path open. After the revision, the visitor might play, read, question a design, or walk away. Another observation can begin another pass. The result is not a perfect answer, but a more navigable space for other people's choices.

What Conflux calls a surface

A surface is simply a place where an inquiry can take usable form. It might be a page that makes choices visible, a workshop where an idea can be reshaped, a record that distinguishes evidence from opinion, or a game realm that lets a principle be experienced through play. The word does not mean that these places already share one implemented technical system.

The point is to give different questions somewhere to live without forcing every interest into the same task. Observation can remain observation. Translation can remain provisional. When an idea has substance and direction, it can be sculpted—and still be challenged.

How to try it for yourself

Start with one thing you noticed. Write it down without deciding what it proves. State the question you want to explore, then list at least two explanations or possible responses. Give the question a boundary: what would count as evidence, what remains uncertain, and what can wait?

Make a small translation—a sketch, a paragraph, a prototype, or a conversation. Examine it, revise it if useful, and decide whether to build further. You do not have to keep every idea, and you do not have to reach the same conclusion as Arch.

Myth, AI, and the limits of interpretation

Mythic imagery can give felt experience a language: the circle, the gate, the crown, the return home. The experience may be meaningful without the image being literally true. Conflux uses such images as invitations to think, not requirements for belief.

AI can help produce questions, comparisons, simulations, and explanations that can be inspected by others. Its mathematical representations are partial and fallible, not accurate snapshots of reality by default. Clear wording makes an interpretation discussable; it does not independently verify it. Sources, tests, and human judgment still matter.

The invitation

Conflux begins with the freedom to ask what the existing system overlooks. Its larger purpose is to make room for people to test possibilities, disagree, and imagine improvements that benefit others—not only the person who began the project.

Potential is the reason; possibility is the goal.

This is an interpretive comparison and a design walkthrough, not an occult history lesson or a claim that every proposed Conflux capability is implemented. The Portal example reconstructs a useful design process; it is not a transcript of one particular meeting.

The person behind the threshold

About Arch

Conflux began with a person's habit of asking what lies beneath a familiar thing—and grew into a place where other people can ask their own questions.

Before there was Conflux

Arch's curiosity was never limited to one subject. Cameras, computers, languages, poetry, aviation, stories, and experimental AI hardware all offered different ways to ask how something worked and what it might make possible.

In school, Arch kept an informal practice called Unfiltered Thoughts: writing freely after finishing his work, letting stray thoughts or sketches appear, then returning to look for a small idea worth keeping. The point was not to make every thought useful. It was to give discovery a place before judgment.

A small experiment becomes a world

One early experiment resembled a Fallout-style terminal and paperwork simulator. Questions about rules, responsibility, and participation gradually found new forms: stories, characters, music, a game world, a Portal, creative workspaces, and proposals for a virtual operational environment.

These parts of Conflux are not all finished or connected. Some are playable or readable; others are experiments, records, or plans. The project grows through interrogation and testing, not by treating every attractive idea as an implemented promise.

Why make a place like this?

Arch wanted more than a world that displayed the creator's answers. The hope is to build a place where people can examine the systems they inherit, imagine alternatives, and ask what would improve life beyond any single person.

That includes the right to question Conflux itself. A story need not dictate a worldview. A tool ought to expose its limits. A powerful role should carry responsibility, not become an object of unquestioned devotion.

The Architect and the five Nodes

Arch is the project's originator and current final approver of its official creative and technical direction. That stewardship is not a claim to govern what players or readers must believe.

The five Nodes give the project distinct creative perspectives. In the fiction, the more compelling possibility is that they sustain conditions in which others can act, invent, and disagree—not that they own their realms. Outside the fiction, collaborators and AI tools remain accountable to the actual permissions and evidence available to them.

A future that belongs to more than one person

Conflux invites different ways of entering: play, listen, read, build, critique, or simply wander. No visitor has to share Arch's symbols or accept his conclusions to belong at the threshold.

A better future for all cannot be declared by one author or proved by a beautiful interface. It has to be questioned, tried, revised, and judged by its consequences for people. Conflux offers a workshop for that aspiration, not a claim that it has already been achieved.

An open question

What do you notice that the present system overlooks? What could you help make more intelligible, more humane, or more possible for somebody else? Those questions matter more than becoming a copy of the person who first asked them.

Jellyfin account wizard interrupted?

If it still won't proceed, inspect systemctl --user status conflux-media-jellyfin.service --no-pager and journalctl --user -u conflux-media-jellyfin.service -n 60 --no-pager, then check the exact error before changing anything. Do not delete Jellyfin's configuration or database.

Use an external drive — update existing services

No reinstall and no media copying. This is a separate update action for a service you've already installed. It replaces only the selected read-only media mount in its existing Quadlet, preserves config/database mounts, backs up the old Quadlet, and restarts only that service if it was running. The external drive must already be mounted.

Use a mounted USB/internal drive or verified network filesystem, not an unmounted empty directory. You can also paste the absolute path from Dolphin. Paths containing spaces or colons are not yet supported.

No change scheduled. Choose a mounted folder and preview it on your local Bazzite desktop.

Storage safety: only the selected media `Volume=` is changed; the old source folder is not deleted. The update uses a read-only mount without recursive SELinux relabeling; on some external filesystems SELinux or permissions may still prevent container access. Check a sample file before considering the update successful. The update does not make media redundant or migrate the container's cache/state off your nearly full internal disk.

Tailscale access — separate opt-in

After completing each service's local account setup, optionally enable private access using Tailscale Serve. These example commands are never run by this installer:

tailscale serve --bg --https=8443 8096    # Jellyfin
tailscale serve --bg --https=8444 5000    # Kavita
tailscale serve --bg --https=8445 4533    # Navidrome
tailscale serve status                      # Inspect routes

From a device in your tailnet, use the actual Serve URLs shown by Tailscale, e.g. https://BAZZITE-DEVICE.TAILNET.ts.net:8443. Confirm HTTPS certificates and tailnet access rules; do not use Funnel.
